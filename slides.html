The Problem

Climate damage isn't rising gently — it's accelerating

  • Climate & weather disasters used to be a line item. Now they reshape budgets.
  • The damage curve bends upward, decade after decade — far faster than population or inflation alone.
  • The bill lands locally: floods, storms, droughts, wildfires hit cities and regions first.

~40s. Hook with the numbers card. $6.3 trillion in inflation-adjusted damage over the record; the planet is ~1.2°C warmer than the 1900s baseline; decade damage grew 4.6× from the 1980s to the 2010s. This is the gap we want a policymaker to feel.

The Data

Two public datasets, one honest correlation

EM-DAT — disaster impacts

  • International Disaster Database (CRED / UCLouvain)
  • Filtered to climate & weather events only (hydro / meteo / climatological)
  • Inflation-adjusted damage, event counts, region, type, year

~1,850 aggregated records · 1900–2026

Global temperature anomaly

  • Observed monthly anomalies → annual mean (1940+)
  • Pre-1940 back-filled from NASA GISTEMP, rebased to the observed series
  • 127 annual points · 1900–2026

Both preprocessed in Python → lightweight JSON

~35s. Why two datasets: one for the cause (temperature), one for the consequence (cost). Stress the climate-only filter — that's a design decision that keeps the correlation legitimate, and it answers a likely Q&A question.

Audience & Goal

For municipal policymakers

Who

City & regional decision-makers who set budgets and plan infrastructure — a specialized audience that can act on detail.

The action we want

Move climate adaptation up the budget agenda now — frame inaction as a compounding cost, not a distant risk.

How we frame it

  • Guided story first — build the cause → consequence argument step by step.
  • Then hand over control — let them interrogate their own region / time range.
  • Concrete money figures, not abstractions.

Design Rationale · Encoding

Why these visuals

  • Area chart for temperature — a continuous, filled trend reads as a single rising force; the 1900s baseline at zero makes "above normal" instantly legible.
  • Bubble map for disasters — area encodes inflation-adjusted damage (perceptually honest via a √-scale); position = geography; colour = disaster type.
  • Stacked bars for the toll — decades on x, damage on y; stacking shows both the exploding total and the type mix in one mark.
  • Shared colour language — the six disaster types use one palette across map, bars and legend, so the eye links them without a key lookup.
~30s. Directly answers the Assignment-2 feedback that rationale was missing. Note the √-scale for area (perceptually correct), zero baseline, and the consistent palette tying the three views together.

Design Rationale · Interaction

Why this flow

Author-driven, then reader-driven

Steps 1–5 are a guided argument (Martini-glass model); step 6 opens into free exploration. Newcomers get the story; experts get the data.

Sticky stage, scrolling text

Two columns: prose on the left drives a fixed visual on the right, so context and chart stay in view together.

Brushing & linking

One brush on the timeline filters both the map and the bars — this is what makes the temperature↔damage correlation explorable, the exact gap the proposal feedback flagged.

Reuse & transitions

Charts persist across steps and animate between states, so the warming line is literally the same object throughout — reinforcing continuity.

~35s. Explains the interaction rationale (other gap from feedback): why guided-then-open, why the brush links all views, why the sticky two-column layout. End of the 5-min demo block.
