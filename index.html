The Baseline: Understanding Global Temperature Records

The period from 1900 to 1929 serves as the baseline (0°C anomaly) against which all subsequent global temperature variations are measured. This era represents the transition between natural climate variability and the onset of anthropogenic warming.

Key Facts

  • Baseline Definition: The global average temperature anomaly from 1900–1929 is set to 0°C for comparison purposes.
  • Data Quality: Global instrumental temperature records become increasingly reliable during this period, particularly after 1910.
  • Industrial Context: The Second Industrial Revolution (1870–1914) had accelerated global emissions, but warming effects were still modest.
  • Natural Variability: Temperature fluctuations during this period reflect natural climate cycles (solar activity, volcanic aerosols, ocean oscillations).

Sources

  • Berkeley Earth Surface Temperature (BEST) Dataset
  • NOAA Global Surface Temperature Analysis
  • Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fifth Assessment Report (AR5)

The Shift: Post-1950 Warming Acceleration

After 1950, the global temperature anomaly departs consistently and markedly above the 1900–1929 baseline. This transition reflects the combined effects of rapid industrialization, energy consumption growth, and a reduction in cooling influences.

What Changed After 1950?

  • Energy Boom: Post-WWII economic expansion drove fossil fuel consumption to unprecedented levels.
  • Atmospheric CO₂: Carbon dioxide concentrations rose from ~310 ppm (1950) to ~330 ppm (1980).
  • Reduced Aerosol Cooling: Air quality improvements in developed nations reduced reflective sulfate aerosols that had masked warming.
  • Ocean Heat Uptake: Oceans began absorbing more heat, though this lagged atmospheric warming by decades.

The Evidence

  • Decadal warming rate: ~0.13°C per decade (1951–2012)
  • Acceleration visible: warming rates doubled after 1980
  • Spatial pattern: Northern Hemisphere warms faster than Southern Hemisphere

Sources

  • IPCC Fifth & Sixth Assessment Reports
  • Morice et al. (2021) - HadCRUT5 Temperature Dataset
  • Masson-Delmotte et al. (2013) - Physical Science Basis

Emergence of Extremes: Early Disaster Patterns (Pre-1990)

The spatial distribution of recorded climate and weather disasters before 1990 reveals early concentrations of climate impacts in vulnerable regions. Each bubble on the map is sized by inflation-adjusted economic loss and colored by hazard type.

Hazard Types & Regional Patterns

  • Floods: Dominant in monsoon regions (South Asia, East Africa, Southeast Asia)
  • Storms: Concentrated in tropical cyclone belts (Western Pacific, Atlantic)
  • Droughts: Recurring in Sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia
  • Wildfires: Emerging threat in Mediterranean and boreal regions
  • Extreme Temperature: Heat waves begin to appear in historical records
  • Landslides: Concentrated in mountainous regions prone to heavy rainfall

Data Considerations

  • Reporting Bias: Wealthier nations report disasters more comprehensively
  • Inflation Adjustment: Economic losses are adjusted to 2020 USD for comparability
  • Undercount: Many small-scale disasters, especially in low-income countries, go unrecorded

Sources

  • EM-DAT: International Disaster Database (Université Catholique de Louvain)
  • Munich Re NatCatSERVICE
  • Swiss Re Institute Reports

The Modern Crisis: Expanding Disaster Footprint (1990–2026)

From 1990 onward, the geographic footprint of recorded climate disasters expands dramatically, and aggregate economic losses accelerate. This reflects both increased event frequency and the growing economic exposure of human settlements.

Key Trends

  • Spatial Expansion: Disasters increasingly affect multiple continents simultaneously (e.g., 2010 Russian heat wave + Pakistan floods + Amazon drought)
  • Frequency Increase: Weather extremes that were once 50-year events now occur every 10–15 years
  • Economic Exposure: Urban expansion puts more assets at risk in coastal and floodplain zones
  • Compound Events: Cascading hazards (drought → wildfire → landslide) become more common

Notable Events (1990–2026)

  • 1998 Indian Summer Monsoon Failure (~2,000+ deaths)
  • 2003 European Heat Wave (~40,000 excess deaths)
  • 2010 Pakistan Mega-Floods (20% of country submerged)
  • 2021 Western North American Heat Dome (record-breaking temperatures)
  • 2022–2024 Global Flood Sequences (Turkey, Pakistan, Brazil, Libya)

Sources

  • EM-DAT International Disaster Database (1900–2026)
  • World Meteorological Organization (WMO) Annual Reports
  • European Environment Agency (EEA) Climate Change Indicators

The Economic Toll: Accelerating Costs of Climate Disasters

The decadal aggregation of economic losses from climate-related disasters reveals an unmistakable trend: annual costs are accelerating, with recent decades experiencing losses orders of magnitude higher than the 20th century baseline.

Cost Breakdown by Hazard Type

  • Floods: ~40% of total losses; dominant in developing regions
  • Storms: ~35% of total losses; increasingly severe in tropical regions
  • Droughts: ~15% of total losses; agriculture-dependent economies most affected
  • Other Hazards: ~10% (wildfires, landslides, extreme heat)

Inflation Adjustment & Real Costs

  • Why Adjust? Direct comparison between 1900 and 2026 losses requires inflation correction to 2020 USD values.
  • Physical Exposure Factor: Beyond inflation, actual exposure has multiplied 10–20× due to urbanization and asset concentration.
  • Underreporting: EM-DAT only records losses above reporting thresholds; many small disasters are missed.

Economic Projections

  • At current warming rates (1.5–2°C), annual losses could exceed $600 billion by 2050
  • Uninsured losses disproportionately affect low-income nations (90% uninsured)
  • Adaptation costs estimated at $500 billion–$2 trillion annually by 2050

Sources

  • EM-DAT Economic Loss Database
  • World Bank: Turn Down the Heat Reports
  • UNISDR: Global Assessment Report on Disaster Risk Reduction (GAR)
  • Munich Re: Natural Catastrophe Reports (2020–2024)

Explore the Data: Interpreting Linked Climate-Disaster Views

This interactive visualization links global temperature trends to recorded disaster patterns. Use the temperature timeline to select different periods and observe how spatial disaster distributions and economic costs correlate with warming regimes.

How to Use the Visualization

  • Brush the Temperature Timeline: Drag the timeline to highlight a specific period (e.g., 1950–1980)
  • View Linked Maps: The disaster map updates to show only events during that period
  • Compare Bar Charts: Decadal loss totals reveal which periods incurred the highest costs
  • Clear Selection: Click outside the brush area to reset and view the full record

Data Integration

  • Temperature Source: Berkeley Earth / NOAA global anomaly records
  • Disaster Source: EM-DAT International Disaster Database
  • Time Alignment: Annual temperature data matched with recorded disaster events
  • Spatial Resolution: Disasters geocoded to country/region centroids

Correlation Insights

  • Pre-1950: Sparse, small-loss events; natural variability dominates
  • 1950–1980: Moderate increase; emergence of regional patterns
  • 1980–2000: Rapid acceleration; clustering visible in monsoon & cyclone regions
  • 2000–2026: Aggressive trend; global geographic spread; record-breaking events

Sources & Methodology

  • Visualization: D3.js v7 with Scrollama.js
  • Data: EM-DAT, Berkeley Earth, NOAA
  • Time Period: 1900–2026 (126 years)
  • Inflation Adjustment: 2020 USD baseline
